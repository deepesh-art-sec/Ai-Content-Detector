I came to study in the United States two years ago from South Korea, and writing academic papers in English was very difficult for me at first.
In my high school in Seoul, we learned a lot of English grammar rules and vocabulary lists, but we rarely wrote long argumentative essays with citations.
When my English professor assigned the first five-page paper about globalization, I spent almost three days translating my thoughts word for word with a dictionary.
My sentences were too short and sometimes the prepositions were wrong, like writing in Monday instead of on Monday or saying depend of instead of depend on.
The tutor at the university writing center was very kind and explained to me that English paragraphs need a clear topic sentence right at the beginning.
She told me not to worry about using fancy big words and instead focus on explaining my ideas clearly so any reader can follow my logic easily.
I wrote my paper about how street food markets in Seoul changed after international tourists started visiting traditional neighborhoods in large numbers.
I interviewed my uncle on KakaoTalk because he has run a small tteokbokki food stall near the Dongdaemun market for more than twenty-five years.
He explained that younger tourists like to take photos for social media, so many shop owners started changing the food presentation and making spicy dishes milder.
Including his personal stories made my paper much more interesting to read than just repeating statistics from government tourism websites.
I was very nervous when the professor handed back our graded papers with red ink comments, but she gave me a B plus and wrote good improvement at the top.
Now I feel much more confident when writing research summaries for my civil engineering courses, even though I still check my spelling carefully before submitting.
When writing our senior design project report on bridge foundations, our team had to follow the state department of transportation formatting manual strictly.
We calculated the soil bearing capacity using standard penetration test data provided by the geotechnical survey firm that drilled the test boreholes last November.
The groundwater table was located four meters below the ground surface, which meant we had to account for buoyancy effects when sizing the concrete footings.
We spent two days modeling the pier geometry in AutoCAD before exporting the three-dimensional mesh into the structural finite element software for load testing.
The wind load calculations took several iterations because the bridge deck is located in an open coastal valley with high seasonal gust factors.
During our mid-semester presentation to the faculty committee, one professor asked why we chose drilled shafts instead of driven steel H-piles for the river piers.
Our team leader explained that drilled shafts produce far less underwater noise and vibration, which was a key environmental requirement for protecting local fish spawning areas.
The department head nodded in agreement and gave us useful suggestions on how to improve our cost comparison table before the final competition in April.
I stayed up late last night proofreading the appendix section to make sure all forty-six calculation sheets were numbered properly and signed off by our faculty advisor.
Our team celebrated after finishing the report by going out for Korean barbecue together and taking a group photo in front of the engineering building.
I sent the photo to my parents back home in Busan, and they called me right away to say they were proud of my hard work this semester.
Graduating with an engineering degree will be a big achievement for my family because neither of my parents had the opportunity to attend college when they were young.
I hope to find a job with a civil engineering consulting firm where I can work on real infrastructure projects that help local communities stay safe from flooding.
