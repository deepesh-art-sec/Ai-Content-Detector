I spent most of Sunday night at the campus library trying to find secondary sources on the labor strikes in Pennsylvania coal towns during the 1920s.
The university archives only had microfiche copies of the local newspapers, which were really blurry and gave me a headache after forty-five minutes of scrolling.
I ended up finding a much better set of interviews in an old oral history journal that someone had returned to the reserve desk earlier that morning.
My history professor told us that the main problem with first drafts is that students try to cover thirty years of events instead of focusing on one specific town.
I decided to narrow my topic down to the union elections in Scranton because the local records gave detailed voting numbers by precinct and immigrant ward.
Writing the introduction was the hardest part because I kept rewriting the opening sentence over and over until it finally sounded like an actual argument.
I talked to my roommate who is majoring in sociology, and she pointed out that religious organizations played just as big a role as political parties in those neighborhoods.
That comment made me rethink my whole third section, so I went back to the stacks to look for parish records from St. Stanislaus church.
When I brought my rough draft to the writing center on Wednesday, the peer tutor suggested cutting out about three paragraphs that wandered off into general background.
Her advice was spot on, and cutting that extra fluff made my central thesis about coal company scrip and housing contracts stand out much more clearly.
Our group project for marketing class was supposed to be a presentation on electric vehicle adoption in rural areas, but nobody could agree on the target demographic.
We ended up having a three-hour meeting in the student center food court over cold pizza before we finally settled on small family farms as our primary case study.
I was in charge of formatting the slides and making sure our citation footnotes were in proper Chicago style with full publication dates and URLs.
During the Q and A session after our talk, one of the business school judges asked a tough question about battery degradation in sub-zero winter temperatures.
Luckily, Dave had read an engineering article about lithium iron phosphate chemistry the night before and was able to give a really solid technical answer.
I honestly think the hardest class this semester has been physical chemistry, mostly because the weekly problem sets take anywhere from eight to twelve hours to finish.
Every Thursday evening about six of us meet up in the study lounge on the third floor of the science center with whiteboards and scrap paper to work through the derivations.
Sometimes we spend twenty minutes arguing over a single minus sign in an integral until someone catches where the integration by parts went sideways.
Walking back to my dorm after midnight in the cold autumn air is always exhausting, but it feels great when you finally understand the thermodynamics behind the equations.
I handed in my final term paper twenty minutes before the deadline on Canvas and then slept for ten hours straight without setting an alarm for once.
My advisor emailed me this morning saying my proposal for summer undergraduate research got approved with a modest stipend from the department endowment.
I will be working alongside two graduate students examining water quality samples collected from the river downstream from the abandoned textile mill.
We have to be at the chemistry building by seven in the morning on sample collection days so we can test the dissolved oxygen levels before the sun warms up the water.
I need to order a pair of rubber waders from the supply catalog before our first field trip out to the marsh next Tuesday.
It is definitely going to be a busy summer, but getting real hands-on field experience is way better than sitting in a lecture hall taking notes all day.
